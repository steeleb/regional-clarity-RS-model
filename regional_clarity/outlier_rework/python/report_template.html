Part 1 · Diagnosis

RANSAC wasn't the culprit

The commit that introduced RANSAC filtering (44546c9, "apply ransac. this is too aggressive") bundled several other new QA filters into the same change, and it turned out those bundled filters — not the RANSAC steps — caused nearly all of the row-count loss.

Finding 1: the SDD-value RANSAC flag was never applied

ransac_site() computes an is_inlier column on the field-measurement (Secchi depth) side, but no chunk in 01_make_matches.Rmd ever filters on it — it's used only in diagnostic plots. It had zero effect on matchup counts before this investigation.

Finding 2: the reflectance-band RANSAC cost only ~3.4%

Comparing the current pipeline's final row count (5,199) against the same pipeline with every QA filter kept except RANSAC (5,381) isolates RANSAC's true cost: about 3.4% of rows, not 70%.

Where the other ~65% actually went

An ablation across the QA filters added in that same commit (5-day window, original full geometry QA) shows scene-level cloud cover was the single biggest cost, followed by metadata QA, with the CV/lake-type filters on the SDD side costing almost nothing:

Finding 3: geometry QA is load-bearing, not a tunable knob

A first attempt at fully removing site geometry QA (shoreline flags + single-waterbody restriction) to recover more rows caused the matching query's output to grow past 25GB within 90 seconds before being killed. Without number_int_wb == 1, one field-monitoring location can join against many ambiguously-assigned, multi-waterbody reflectance candidates — a combinatorial blowup, not a data-quality nicety. Every configuration tested after that keeps this constraint.

Part 2 · Redesign

Final outlier/QA pipeline

QA loosening alone (keeping the original full geometry QA, dropping cloud QA entirely) tops out at 6,839 rows at a 5-day window — short of the 10k floor. Widening the match window to ~16–20 days would clear it, but that was ruled out to keep match-time precision at the original 5 days. The lever that actually got there: relaxing geometry QA to just number_int_wb == 1, dropping the shoreline-proximity flags on the reasoning that near-shore contamination is already handled upstream in the siteSR remote-sensing pipeline.

  1. Region prefilter (HUC2) — unchanged, free narrowing. Nothing outside these 7 HUC2 basins can reach the final 6-state output anyway.
  2. Match window: 5 days — unchanged, explicit constraint. Not treated as a tunable lever here, even though widening it was the only way to clear 10k under the original geometry QA.
  3. Geometry QA relaxed to number_int_wb == 1 only — shoreline flags dropped. Shoreline/near-shore contamination QA already happens upstream in the siteSR pipeline; re-filtering on it here was redundant. number_int_wb is kept — it's what actually prevents the join blowup.
  4. Metadata QA unchanged (pCount ratio > 0.5, pCount > 8, per-pixel prop_clouds == 0). Cheap (~6% of geometry-only rows) and legitimate; the per-pixel cloud check here already targets contamination at the specific site.
  5. Scene cloud-cover threshold relaxed 20% → 40% — kept, not dropped. With geometry QA relaxed there's enough headroom (11,693 rows measured at 20%) to keep a real scene-level cloud filter rather than removing it.
  6. SDD-value RANSAC now actually wired up as a filter — gently: only confirmed outliers (is_inlier == FALSE) are dropped; indeterminate (too-few-observations, NA) sites are kept. Previously computed but never applied. Dropped 11,777 / 1,654,029 rows (0.71%).
  7. Reflectance-band RANSAC rebuilt as majority-vote scoring instead of winner-take-all, with missing (location, scene) lookups defaulting to "keep." The original picked the single best-fitting random iteration per site, which is sensitive to one lucky/unlucky draw. Voting across all iterations (same design as the SDD-side filter) is far more stable. Retained 96.3% of candidate rows.
Result

12,659 final training rows (region-filtered, closest-observation-deduplicated, lake/reservoir-only), up from 5,199 on the current main branch — a 143.6% increase, comfortably above the 10k floor, at the original 5-day match window.

Part 3 · Modeling

Model comparison methodology

All three models are trained and evaluated under the exact spatial holdout scheme from 03_split_data.Rmd: 5 partitions built by greedily bin-packing HUC4 basins (never splitting a basin across partitions) into roughly equal-sized groups. Partitions 1–4 form a leave-one-partition-out cross-validation loop (train on 3, validate on the 4th, rotate); partition 5 is held out entirely and touched exactly once, at the end, for final evaluation.

No-leakage discipline

Partition 5 is never used for feature selection, hyperparameter search, or model choice — only for the one final scored prediction per model. Correlation-based feature pruning is fit on partitions 1–4 only. The NN's imputation and standardization are fit per-fold on that fold's training split only, never on validation/test rows. time_diff (days between the field sample and the satellite pass) was excluded from the candidate feature set entirely — it doesn't exist at deployment time when scoring an arbitrary satellite pass with no paired field sample, so a model trained on it would learn something it can never use in production.

Feature set

58 candidate features (7 Gardner-corrected reflectance bands + 50 band-ratio/normalized-difference spectral indices, replicated from 04_make_models.Rmd, + the LaSRC/LEDAPS atmospheric-correction indicator) were reduced to 39 by correlation-cluster pruning (|r| > 0.95 connected components, keeping the feature most correlated with Secchi depth in each cluster), computed on the training/CV partitions only.

Three things stand out:

  1. Partition 3 is hard for every model architecture, not just one. All three independently-tuned models show their largest train-val gap and worst R² on partition 3 (and partition 4 for the NN). If any single model were simply memorizing its training data, the hard fold would be architecture-specific; instead the same partition is hard across a shallow tree ensemble, a different shallow tree ensemble, and a neural net. That points to genuine spatial heterogeneity - partition 3's HUC4 basins likely differ systematically from the others - rather than overfitting.
  2. Partition 1 shows a negative gap for every model. Validation RMSE is lower than training RMSE on the easiest partition for all three models. Overfitting produces a positive gap by definition (worse on unseen data); a negative gap on one fold and a large positive gap on another is the signature of uneven partition difficulty, not a leaky or memorizing model.
  3. The hyperparameter search itself pushed toward simple models, which is reassuring but not a substitute for the check above. XGBoost's winning config uses max_depth=2 (3-leaf trees) with colsample_bytree=0.5; LightGBM's uses num_leaves=7 with colsample_bytree=0.5 - both are heavily regularized by construction, since a deeper/wider config would have scored worse on the CV search that selected them. The NN is the exception worth flagging: its winning config (64-64-32 hidden layers ≈ 8,800 parameters against ~7,600 training rows per fold - roughly 1 parameter per training row) has weight_decay=0 (no L2 penalty), relying on dropout (0.3) and early stopping alone. That's consistent with the NN also having the largest fold-to-fold variance (val RMSE sd=0.504, vs. 0.312 for XGBoost and 0.303 for LightGBM) and the worst held-out test performance of the three - it's the one model where "somewhat overparameterized for this dataset size" is a fair characterization, and it's the weakest performer for exactly that reason.
Bottom line

The CV-to-test RMSE gap reported in Part 4 is better explained by spatial heterogeneity across HUC4 partitions (visible consistently across all three architectures, and consistent with the HUC-1009 outlier already visible in the residuals-by-HUC4 figure) than by overfitting. LightGBM is the most stable model across folds as well as the most accurate on average - a second, independent reason to prefer it over XGBoost or the NN.

Part 4b · Refinement

Fixing the ceiling effect: SDD-weighted training

The pred-vs-observed figure above shows the same pattern flagged in the existing 04_make_models.Rmd pipeline: squared-error loss is dominated by the dense cluster of low/mid-clarity observations, so the sparse high-clarity tail is never pushed hard during training. Part 5 · Extending the feature set

Do site characteristics or antecedent weather help?

Topp et al. 2021 (Environ. Res. Lett. 16 055025) paired 3 optical variables with 8 static LakeCat catchment variables for a national Secchi-depth model - notably, not antecedent weather: their weather/climate variables (temperature, precipitation, PDO) were used only in a separate trend-correlation analysis, never as a model input. This section tests both anyway: 7 site characteristics (elevation via 3DEP/AWS terrain tiles, plus LakeCat catchment land cover - imperviousness, urban/forest/cropland/wetland %, matching Topp's variable categories at the same 2006 vintage) and 20 antecedent-weather summaries (gridMET precip/max-temp/min-temp/solar radiation over the 1/3/7/30 days before each satellite pass, the same "previous days" convention used elsewhere in this group's pipelines), added to the existing optical baseline and run through the identical spatial-CV, correlation-pruning, and SDD-weighted-training pipeline as every other result in this report.

wb_nhd_id → LakeCat COMID join, verified

Before trusting this join, its provenance was checked directly: the upstream AquaMatch_siteSR_WQP pipeline sets wb_nhd_id from either NHDPlusV2's comid (CONUS, HUC4 < 1900) or NHD Best-Resolution's permanent_identifier (non-CONUS) depending on region - two different ID spaces. All 1,525 sites in this study carry wb_nhd_source == "NHDPlusv2" (our 7-HUC2 region never reaches the non-CONUS branch), and a fresh, independent nhdplusTools::get_waterbodies() lookup on a real study site (Utah Lake) returned an exact COMID and area match against its wb_nhd_id.

Three findings, each holding consistently across both XGBoost and LightGBM:

  1. Site characteristics are the bigger lever, and they're not redundant with the optical signal. RMSE drops 1.5-3.4%, R² rises by ~0.035-0.051, and all 7 offered site features (elevation, catchment area, and all 5 land-cover percentages) survive |r|>0.95 correlation pruning for every model - none of them are close substitutes for a spectral band or index.
  2. ...but site characteristics come with a real cost: bias flips sign and grows several-fold in magnitude, from small-and-negative to +0.24-0.27 m (~7-8% of the mean SDD). This shows up in the pooled CV metrics (parts 1-4) as well as the held-out test partition - it's not a partition-5 fluke. The likely mechanism: these are static, per-site features (identical for every observation at a given lake), and SDD-weighted training already trades low/mid-range accuracy for high-SDD accuracy (Part 4b); giving the model a persistent per-lake signal appears to let it lean harder into that trade, overcorrecting upward more broadly.
  3. Antecedent weather helps more modestly (~0.5-1.7% RMSE) and doesn't reintroduce that bias - bias stays within ±0.04 m for both tree models, the smallest-magnitude bias of any non-baseline configuration. 13 of the 20 candidate weather features survive pruning (all four precip windows, most temperature windows, most solar-radiation windows), suggesting genuine, broadly-distributed signal rather than one or two lucky features.

Combining both gets the best RMSE/R² of everything tried in this investigation for the two boosted-tree models - XGBoost, optical+site+weather, SDD-weighted: RMSE 1.751 m, R² 0.507 - but inherits roughly the same elevated bias as site-characteristics-alone (+0.19 m), since weather doesn't fully cancel out whatever site characteristics are doing to the bias. The neural net doesn't follow this pattern: site characteristics alone still help it, but weather alone hurts it, and combining the two leaves it slightly worse than the optical baseline (2.019 vs. 1.971 m) - consistent with Part 3b's finding that it's the one relatively overparameterized model here, extra features seem to cost it more than they're worth.

Which to ship depends on what you're optimizing for

Lowest RMSE/highest R²: XGBoost, optical+site+weather (1.751 m, R²=0.507, bias +0.192 m). Lowest bias: LightGBM, optical+weather (1.822 m, R²=0.458, bias –0.029 m) or the original LightGBM, optical-only (1.853 m, bias –0.031 m) from Part 4b. If this model feeds trend detection or change-over-time analysis, a consistent, near-zero bias matters more than a marginal RMSE gain; if it feeds single-observation clarity estimates, the lower-RMSE option may be worth the bias trade. This wasn't decided here - it's a call for whoever sets the model's actual use case.

Part 5b · Mid-investigation correction

A weather-window bug, and a new outlier filter

Two issues surfaced during review of the feature-engineering above, both addressed before any further work: an asymmetry in how the antecedent-weather windows were anchored, and a new outlier-removal filter for large rain events between the image and field dates. Both are upstream of every model in this report - fixing them meant regenerating the weather data and re-running the entire modeling pipeline (all numbers above already reflect the fix).

Weather-window anchor bug

The antecedent-weather windows (Part 5's "previous N days") were originally computed as "N days before the satellite image," full stop. The +-5 day match window is close to a 50/50 split on which side leads (5,360 image-before-field vs. 6,384 image-after-field observations) - when the field sample came after the image, that window never reaches forward to the field date at all, silently missing the image-to-field gap for essentially half the dataset. Fixed by anchoring each window on max(image_date, field_date) instead, which always covers the full gap regardless of which one led. A proposed alternative - anchoring on the field date always, plus a before/after-overpass categorical - was considered and set aside: at deployment there's no field date to anchor to or compare against (the model scores a bare satellite image), so a model trained on that definition would be fed a systematically different feature at inference time. The max() anchor has no such train/deploy mismatch, since it collapses to the image date whenever no field date exists.

New filter: heavy single-day rain between image and field dates

A large rain event between the image and field dates - in either order - means the reflectance and the target describe genuinely different water states, independent of which one came first. Flagged on peak single-day precipitation within the gap (not cumulative gap-total, which conflates a real storm with a longer gap just having more days to accumulate ordinary rain) exceeding the standard NOAA/EPA "heavy precipitation day" threshold of 25.4 mm (1 inch) in 24 hours - a flat, externally-grounded cut, not a percentile of this dataset, and no regional normalization since this dataset is overwhelmingly semi-arid Mountain West sites where 25.4 mm is already unusual almost everywhere in it.

Result: 22 of 12,659 observations flagged (0.17%), spread across 11 different HUC4 basins with no single basin losing more than 2.4% of its rows - exactly the small, distributed effect expected going in. Final training set: 12,637 rows, still comfortably above the 10k floor from Part 2.

Part 7 · Feature reduction, round 1

Is flat correlation pruning splitting decisions it shouldn't?

Every model in this report so far used the same redundancy-pruning method: |Pearson r| > 0.95 connected components, keeping the feature most correlated with target per cluster. That method has a known failure mode - "chaining": if A correlates with B, and B with C, they all land in one cluster even if A and C barely correlate with each other. Two alternatives were run side by side on train_val only (never the test partition) to check whether this was actually costing anything here:

flat, two-pass
the current method, applied a second time to its own survivors - catches cluster-winners that are still correlated with each other post-pruning
hierarchical
average-linkage clustering on 1−max(|Pearson r|, |Spearman ρ|), cut at the same 0.95 threshold - average linkage requires every pair in a cluster to clear the threshold, not just a chain of neighbors; the Spearman term also catches monotonic-but-nonlinear redundancy Pearson alone can miss

Two-pass flat pruning never changes anything here - every survivor of the first pass is already mutually below the 0.95 threshold, so there's no chaining artifact within this correlation structure for the flat method to catch on a second pass. Hierarchical clustering does differ, in two distinct ways:

Optical: single-band ratios vs. 2-band-sum ratios

In every feature group, hierarchical clustering drops the same 9 single-band ratios flat pruning keeps (BN, BR, GN, NDSSI, NR, NmR, RN, RS, SN) in favor of 5 different features (B_RG, G_NS, N_GS, R_NS, blue_corr7). This is a real, not cosmetic, difference in what gets modeled: the single-band ratios are the classic, standard water-color indices (blue/red and NIR/red ratios are textbook turbidity/chlorophyll proxies); the 2-band-sum ratios hierarchical prefers are custom combinations with no equivalent standing in the water-quality remote sensing literature. Hierarchical's picks may be marginally tighter statistically in this dataset - the tie-break rule is "most correlated with target," which doesn't know or care about conventional naming - but that's a real interpretability cost, not a free win. Worth deciding deliberately rather than by default before this goes further: keep the tie-break as pure target-correlation, or bias it toward named/conventional indices when the correlation gap between candidates is small?

Antecedent weather: which windows actually survive, and does the 5-day match window make that reasonable?

For the weather-inclusive groups, hierarchical additionally keeps 4 temperature-window features flat pruning treats as redundant (tmax_degC_prev3, tmean_degC_prev1, tmean_degC_prev7, tmin_degC_prev3), balancing out its optical-side reduction to an identical final count - a coincidence in the total, not in which features survive.

On whether the short (1/3-day) vs. long (7/30-day) windows are mechanistically reasonable given the up-to-5-day gap between image and field dates: the literature doesn't give one clean answer, but it does bound the question usefully. This project's own underlying dataset, AquaSat, uses a ±1 day matchup window as its primary standard; related work (LAGOS-US LANDSAT) goes out to ±7 days. This project's 5-day window sits inside that established range - tighter than LAGOS, looser than AquaSat's strict standard - so it isn't an outlier choice, but it does mean short antecedent-weather windows (computed relative to the image date) can end up describing conditions several days removed from the actual field measurement when the gap runs toward the 5-day end, which is exactly the failure mode the anchor-date fix in Part 5b addresses for the window's endpoint, though not its length. Separately, storm-driven turbidity in streams peaks and largely clears within hours to a couple of days of the event itself, while once sediment reaches a lake it can take much longer to settle out - one study of an oil sands pit lake reported roughly a 25-day e-folding decay time for elevated epilimnetic turbidity. That asymmetry argues both windows are picking up something real rather than noise: short windows (1/3-day) are closer to "was there an active runoff pulse," longer windows (7/30-day) closer to "how much sediment is still suspended from whatever fell recently" - genuinely different signals, not redundant ones, which is consistent with hierarchical clustering finding more of them worth keeping separately than flat pruning did.

Where this leaves the plan

Hierarchical clustering is doing real work, not just relabeling the same decision - it changes which specific features are offered to every downstream model, in a way that's defensible on both statistical grounds (average linkage vs. chaining) and, for the weather windows, on physical grounds. The open call is the optical tie-break (interpretability vs. raw target-correlation) flagged above. Next: cross-fold-consensus backward elimination (Part 3b's already-established technique, automated with a shadow/permuted-decoy stopping rule) on top of whichever pruning method is chosen, run per model, plus a shared-feature benchmark arm for a fair cross-model comparison - as scoped earlier, pending the tie-break decision.

Part 8 · Limitations & next steps

What to check before trusting this in production

  1. Spot-check the relaxed geometry QA on a sample of newly-admitted near-shore sites. This investigation trusted that upstream siteSR QA handles shoreline contamination; that assumption hasn't been directly verified against the new site set.
  2. The high-Secchi ceiling effect is mitigated, not solved, by SDD-weighted training (Part 4b). Weighting reduces bias substantially and improves top-quartile RMSE in all three models, but the pred-vs-observed ceiling is still visible - a bigger structural fix (e.g. a log-transformed target, quantile loss, or a two-stage model) may be worth trying if high-clarity accuracy matters most for the end use case.
  3. Investigate HUC 1009's larger residual spread specifically before deploying basin-level predictions there.
  4. Decide the RMSE-vs-bias tradeoff from Part 5 before picking a final model - it wasn't resolved here on purpose. Also worth trying: diagnosing exactly why site characteristics shift bias upward (per-HUC4 bias breakdown, the way Part 4 already does for the baseline model) rather than the plausible-but-unconfirmed mechanism proposed in Part 5; and testing whether it's specific to SDD-weighted training or shows up unweighted too.
  5. Feature reduction is in progress, not finished (Part 7): the pruning-method comparison is done, but cross-fold-consensus backward elimination (with a shadow/permuted-decoy stopping rule), per-model vs. shared feature sets, and a shared-feature benchmark arm are still scoped, not run.
  6. Re-render the tracked pipeline docs (01_make_matches.Rmd through 04_make_models.Rmd) with the new parameters once these are reviewed, since this investigation ran as standalone scripts in regional_clarity/outlier_rework/ rather than editing the Rmd pipeline in place.
