Training data

12,637 field–satellite matchups

Each training row pairs a field-measured Secchi depth with the closest Landsat overpass within a 5-day window, restricted to lakes and reservoirs in the 6-state region. Four filtering stages keep this matched set both large and trustworthy:

  1. Site geometry. Candidate sites are restricted to those intersecting exactly one waterbody (number_int_wb == 1) - required for the match to be spatially unambiguous, since a site touching more than one waterbody can't be reliably assigned to either. Near-shore contamination is handled upstream, in the siteSR remote-sensing pipeline that produces the candidate reflectance data, so no separate shoreline-distance filter is applied here.
  2. Metadata and cloud quality. Candidate scenes require majority-clear water pixels in the site buffer (pCount_dswe1/pCount_dswe_gt0 > 0.5, pCount_dswe1 > 8) and zero cloud-flagged pixels at the site itself (prop_clouds == 0) - a targeted, per-pixel cloud check rather than a coarser whole-scene threshold.
  3. Reflectance-outlier filtering. A per-site RANSAC-style filter on the six corrected reflectance bands plus surface temperature flags scenes whose values are inconsistent with that site's own trend line. Each candidate point is scored by the fraction of random trend-line fits that classify it as an inlier (majority-vote across iterations, not a single best-fitting draw), with points inside the range already spanned by accepted values at that site never flagged regardless of vote share.
  4. Heavy-rain gap filter. A matchup is excluded if peak single-day precipitation between the image date and the field-sample date - in either order - reaches 25.4 mm (1 inch/24hr, the standard NOAA/EPA "heavy precipitation day" threshold): a rain event of that size between the two dates means the reflectance and the target value describe genuinely different water states. 22 of 12,659 candidate matchups (0.17%) are excluded on this basis, spread across 11 HUC4 basins with no single basin losing more than 2.4% of its rows.

Modeling methodology

Spatial holdout cross-validation, three architectures

Training rows are grouped into 5 partitions by greedily bin-packing HUC4 basins into equal-sized groups, never splitting a basin across partitions. Partitions 1–4 form a leave-one-partition-out cross-validation loop; partition 5 is held out entirely and scored exactly once, at the end. A model's final prediction on partition 5 is the average of 4 fold models (one per CV rotation), matching the ensemble structure already used by the project's existing XGBoost pipeline (04_make_models.Rmd) - no single model is ever fit on all of partitions 1–4 combined, since doing so would leave no independent data to early-stop against.

XGBoost
25-trial random search · gbtree, squared error · early stopping on CV RMSE
LightGBM
25-trial random search · regression/RMSE objective · early stopping
Neural net
15-trial random search · 1–3 hidden layers, ≤8 units each, ReLU + dropout · Adam, per-fold median-impute + standardize

Candidate features are reduced by correlation-cluster pruning (|Pearson r| > 0.95 connected components, keeping the feature most correlated with target per cluster) fit on partitions 1–4 only. All hyperparameter search, feature selection, and training use only partitions 1–4; partition 5 is touched once, for the final reported metric.

The neural net's search space is deliberately small - at most 3 hidden layers of at most 8 units each - sized to the dataset rather than to a generic MLP default. With 13–44 candidate features and roughly 10k training rows, a wider network has far more capacity than the problem supports and no structural regularization to fall back on, unlike the tree models' built-in feature subsampling.

Partitions 3 and 4 are consistently the hardest folds: both tree models show their single largest train-val gap on partition 3 (XGBoost +0.809, LightGBM +0.691), and the neural net shows its two largest gaps split almost evenly between partition 3 (+0.451) and partition 4 (+0.488) - the same two partitions standing out for every architecture, which points to genuine spatial heterogeneity in those partitions' HUC4 basins rather than any one model memorizing its training data. The neural net's higher fold-to-fold variance, despite its small size, is consistent with it having the least structural regularization of the three architectures - it has no built-in feature or row subsampling to fall back on the way the tree ensembles do.

SDD-weighted training

Correcting the high-clarity ceiling

Squared-error loss is dominated by the dense cluster of low/mid-clarity observations, so the sparse high-clarity tail is never pushed hard during ordinary training. Each model is retrained with a per-fold weight that scales linearly with observed Secchi depth (the highest-SDD training point counts 3× as much as the lowest, k=2), using the same tuned hyperparameters as the unweighted model.

Weighting helps the tree models, not the neural net

For XGBoost and LightGBM, SDD-weighting reproduces the same tradeoff in both: top-quartile RMSE improves (XGBoost 3.054→2.945, LightGBM 3.078→2.960) and bias improves in every slice, at a modest cost to low/mid-range RMSE. The right-sized neural net shows the opposite pattern - weighting very slightly worsens every metric in every slice, including the top quartile it's meant to help (RMSE 3.116→3.151). At 3 layers of at most 8 units, the network has little slack capacity left to trade off: it is already close to its ceiling on the dominant low/mid-range cluster, so reweighting the loss doesn't free up capacity for the tail the way it does for the higher-capacity tree ensembles. SDD-weighted training is retained for the tree models and not applied to the neural net's reported numbers elsewhere in this report.

Site characteristics help every model, on their own: RMSE improves by 0.06–0.07 m for all three, the single most reliable gain of any feature-group addition in this comparison. Antecedent weather is more architecture-dependent. Added to the tree models, it gives a small RMSE improvement on its own and, combined with site characteristics, produces each tree model's best overall configuration - XGBoost with the full optical+site+weather feature set is the best single result in this study, RMSE 1.747 m, R²=0.508. Added to the capped neural net, weather does not help: both optical+weather and optical+site+weather score worse than optical+site alone, and worse than optical alone in optical+weather's case. The neural net's own best configuration is optical+site (RMSE 1.795), not the full feature set - consistent with its narrower capacity leaving less room to benefit from the larger, more redundant weather block once site characteristics are already included.

Across every model and every configuration, optical features carry the majority of attribution - never less than 64%, and 100% by construction in the optical-only configuration. Site characteristics are a real secondary contributor (25–29% when present): catchment area and land-cover percentages consistently rank just behind the leading blue/green and red-band features. Weather is consistently the smallest contributor (7–14%), and never displaces optical or site features from the top of the ranking.

Does weather behave physically sensibly?

Correlating each weather feature's raw value against its own SHAP value checks direction, not just magnitude: a negative correlation means higher precipitation or temperature pushes the prediction toward lower clarity, the expected physical relationship. The month-scale signals are the cleanest: precip_mm_prev30 and tmax_degC_prev30 are negative in essentially every model and feature-group combination that includes them, consistent with sustained rainfall and heat reducing clarity through runoff, resuspension, and algal growth.

One feature is consistently counter-intuitive - tmin_degC_prev7 shows a positive value-SHAP correlation in every configuration that includes it, the opposite of the naive physical expectation. A few shorter-window features (precip_mm_prev1, solar radiation at the 1- vs. 7-day windows) flip sign between configurations rather than settling on one direction. The weather feature set is itself moderately intercorrelated (daily temperature and radiation variables move together across the 1/3/7/30-day windows), which is the most likely explanation: SHAP apportions credit among correlated features in a way that can flip an individual feature's sign even when the group's net effect is physically sensible. This is a genuine caveat on individual short-window weather-feature interpretation, not on the model's overall behavior - the month-scale precipitation and max-temperature signals, which carry the clearest independent physical meaning, are also the most consistently correctly signed.

Framing conclusion

This model is primarily optical, secondarily site-informed, and weather-refined - not a weather model fine-tuned by satellite. Band reflectance and derived indices set the majority of every prediction; catchment characteristics add a real, physically interpretable correction (larger catchments and different land cover shift expected clarity); antecedent weather adds a smaller, directionally-mostly-sensible refinement on top. Adding site and weather features earns their keep in test RMSE without changing what kind of model this is.

Feature reduction

Correlation pruning: flat vs. hierarchical

Two pruning strategies were compared on the curated candidate set, both fit on partitions 1–4 only: a flat method (connected components on pairs with |Pearson r| > 0.95, one or two passes) and a hierarchical method (average-linkage clustering on 1−max(|Pearson r|, |Spearman ρ|), cut at the same 0.95 threshold). The flat method can chain features together through an intermediate neighbor even when the endpoints aren't themselves highly correlated; average linkage requires every pair within a cluster to clear the threshold.

With the curated feature set, the two methods now mostly agree. For the optical group, they differ by only one feature each way (flat drops blue_corr7 that hierarchical keeps; hierarchical drops NR and fai that flat keeps) - a much smaller, lower-stakes disagreement than the pre-curation comparison, since removing the ad-hoc ratio features also removed most of the chaining that made the two methods diverge. The clearest difference is in the weather-inclusive groups: hierarchical pruning keeps several additional temperature-window features (tmax_degC_prev3, tmean_degC_prev1, tmean_degC_prev7, tmin_degC_prev3) that the flat method chains away, because those features are each highly correlated with a common neighbor without being highly correlated with each other - exactly the pattern hierarchical pruning is designed to catch. The flat single-pass method is used for the model comparisons in this report; it is marginally more aggressive and, on this feature set, the choice barely matters.

Next steps

Formal feature pruning

Correlation pruning removes redundancy but doesn't test whether a surviving feature actually helps. The next phase is cross-fold-consensus backward elimination: features are dropped one at a time based on their impact across all 4 CV folds simultaneously (not just one), stopped by comparison against a shadow/permuted decoy feature rather than a fixed feature count. This phase will also build the shared-feature benchmark - one feature set forced across all three model architectures - to compare against the per-model-optimized sets used throughout this report.

Two decisions from this report carry forward into that phase, on domain-expertise grounds rather than pure correlation statistics:

  1. Weather candidates narrow to two. Of the 20 antecedent-weather candidates, only precip_mm_prev30 and tmax_degC_prev30 showed a consistently, physically sensible SHAP direction across every model and configuration. The remaining 18 - including the shorter 1/3/7-day windows and the less consistent 30-day temperature/radiation variables - are dropped from the candidate pool before backward elimination begins, rather than left for correlation pruning or the elimination process to sort out. Why: a variable whose sign flips depending on which other features happen to be in the model isn't a reliable input to hand a backward-elimination search, whatever its raw correlation with the target.
  2. Flat single-pass correlation pruning, not hierarchical. The two methods compared earlier in this report agree closely on this feature set; flat single-pass is the simpler method and is carried forward as the correlation-reduction step feeding into backward elimination.
